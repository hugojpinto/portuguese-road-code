Roundabouts: PT 2014 lane rule vs lane integrity

On a two-lane roundabout, does "pick the lane by your exit, and the outer lane must exit" make things safer, or does it create in-ring lane changes, one-sided queues and grey-area side impacts?

How the model works, and its limits

  • Geometry: a four-arm roundabout about 64 m across, with two circulating lanes (centre-lines at 26 m and 30 m radius), two-lane entries and two-lane exits. Traffic circulates counter-clockwise.
  • Who is in which lane is decided by geometry. A car cutting across the outer lane is "in" the outer lane exactly while its body is there, so outer-lane drivers see it and brake (after their reaction time). Collisions are detected from the actual car footprints. No conflict type is scripted.
  • Giving way: entering cars and cars leaving from the inner lane use a conflict-zone gap test, re-checked continuously until they can no longer stop. Drivers can miss a car (more often in the blind spot) and can misread signals. Under PT 2014 they may also assume an outer-lane car will exit because the rule says it must.
  • Same drivers, same human-error rates, same random traffic for every rule. Only lane choice, in-ring behaviour, and what drivers expect of others change.
  • Absolute crash rates are inflated so crashes are frequent enough to compare. Only the ratios between rules mean anything.
  • Error-free check: with distraction and perception errors switched off, crashes still happen, at about 11 (lane integrity), 23 (PT 2014 ideal) and 42 (PT 2014 as practised) per 1000 vehicles. Part of this baseline is imperfection in the give-way model itself, and it affects every rule. The extra under PT 2014 comes mainly from cars moving out to the outer lane (as the rule requires) exactly where the next arm's entrants merge in, and, in practice, from drivers trusting that outer-lane cars will exit. Read small differences with caution.
  • Not modelled: pedestrians, cyclists, heavy vehicles straddling lanes, and the PT 2014 exemption that lets heavy vehicles and cyclists use the outer lane for any exit. The turbo variant is a simplified symmetric spiral; real turbo roundabouts are asymmetric (main vs side road) and slow traffic further with tighter radii, which isn't modelled. Crashed cars are removed instantly, which slightly relieves congestion.

Annex A: Legal context

How the rule sets in this simulator map to real legislation. There is no EU-level harmonisation: driving-behaviour rules are national, under the broad frame of the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic.

For any other exit, only occupy the rightmost lane after passing the exit before yours, moving over progressively. Signalling the exit is mandatory.
